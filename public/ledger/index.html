FIELD NOTE 001 / SYSTEMS / PUBLIC

Start with the workflow.

A useful automation starts with a clear account of what happens now. Where does a request arrive? Who decides what it means? What information gets copied, lost, or requested twice?

Map one complete journey before choosing a tool. A missed handoff can matter more than a slow individual step. Automating that slow step alone may only deliver work faster to the same bottleneck.

A practical starting point

  1. Choose one repeated process with a clear beginning and end.
  2. Record the inputs, decisions, handoffs, and exceptions.
  3. Identify one improvement you can observe: fewer missed requests, less re-entry, or a shorter turnaround.
  4. Test the smallest change that could produce it.

The first goal is understanding. The right implementation follows from that.

FIELD NOTE 002 / VENTURES / PUBLIC

Give every experiment a boundary.

An idea becomes easier to evaluate when the test has limits. Decide what you need to learn, how much time you will spend, and what result would justify another step.

“Build a platform” is a direction. “See whether five operators share this problem and how they solve it today” is an experiment. The second produces a decision before the first consumes months.

Write the decision before the test

State the assumption, the evidence you need, and the condition for continuing. Include a reason to stop. An experiment that disproves an assumption has done useful work.

Keep the next commitment proportionate to what you know. Discovery, prototype, and launch are different stages; each should earn the next.

FIELD NOTE 003 / OPERATIONS / PUBLIC

Build for the handoff.

A system is not finished when its creator can operate it. It needs to make sense to the person who inherits it, including when something goes wrong.

Useful documentation explains the purpose, the owner, the normal path, and the recovery path. Keep those details close to the work and simple enough to stay current.

Four questions to leave answered

  • What is this system responsible for?
  • Who notices and responds if it stops working?
  • Where can someone verify the result?
  • How can the change be paused or reversed?

Continuity is built through clear ownership and repeatable practice. Documentation is part of the product.
